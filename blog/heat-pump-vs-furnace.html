Heat Pump vs. Gas Furnace: Which Is Right for Your Springfield Home?

May 5, 2025  ·  Bryan Swanson  ·  8 min read

If you're shopping for a new HVAC system, you've probably heard about heat pumps — and maybe noticed that opinions on them are surprisingly strong. Some contractors push them hard; others say they're not right for Missouri. Here's an honest breakdown to help you decide.

How Heat Pumps Work

A heat pump doesn't generate heat by burning fuel — it moves heat from one place to another. In summer, it works like a conventional AC, moving heat from inside your home to outside. In winter, it reverses and extracts heat from outdoor air (even cold air contains usable heat) and moves it inside.

This process is highly efficient — heat pumps can deliver 2–4 units of heat energy for every 1 unit of electrical energy consumed. That's why their efficiency ratings (measured in HSPF2) can seem impossibly high.

The Missouri Climate Problem

Here's where it gets real: traditional heat pumps lose efficiency as temperatures drop. Old-technology heat pumps below 35°F might barely heat your home efficiently, and below 20°F they'd struggle to keep up at all without backup heat.

Springfield, MO averages 15–20 days per year below 25°F. This used to be a significant strike against heat pumps here.

Cold-Climate Heat Pumps Change the Equation

Modern "cold-climate" heat pumps (like the Rheem EcoNet Platinum Series) are engineered to operate efficiently down to -13°F to -22°F. These units can often replace gas heat entirely — even in Missouri winters.

The trade-off: they cost more upfront than conventional heat pumps, and electricity costs more per BTU than natural gas in most scenarios.

Cost Comparison: Heat Pump vs. Gas Furnace

Initial cost: A complete heat pump system (replacing both your AC and furnace) typically runs $8,000–$16,000 installed. A gas furnace + separate AC system runs $7,000–$14,000 installed. Heat pumps cost a bit more upfront.

Operating cost: With current Springfield utility rates, a high-efficiency gas furnace (95–98% AFUE) is generally cheaper to operate than even a high-efficiency heat pump during Missouri winters. But the gap is narrowing as electricity rates stabilize and gas prices fluctuate.

Federal tax credits: Through 2032, cold-climate heat pumps qualify for a 30% federal tax credit (up to $2,000). This can significantly close the initial cost gap.

When a Heat Pump Makes Sense

  • You don't have existing gas service (all-electric home)
  • You want to reduce carbon footprint and electricity is sourced from renewables
  • You're in a milder microclimate within Missouri
  • You want one system that handles both heating and cooling with one set of maintenance
  • You qualify for utility rebates or the federal tax credit

When a Gas Furnace Makes More Sense

  • You already have gas service and your bills are manageable
  • You want the lowest monthly operating cost in Missouri winters
  • Budget is tight and you want the lowest upfront cost
  • Your home is large and you need high heating capacity

Our Recommendation for Springfield

There's no single right answer. For most Springfield homeowners with existing gas service, a high-efficiency gas furnace paired with a high-SEER AC still offers the best combination of upfront cost and operating economy. But for the right home — especially all-electric homes or those prioritizing efficiency and sustainability — a modern cold-climate heat pump is a legitimate and smart choice.
